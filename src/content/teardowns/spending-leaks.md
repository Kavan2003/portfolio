The question: is a tool that tracks recurring "spending leaks" an urgent must-have or a nice-to-have for young tech professionals in India?

To find out, I ran a LinkedIn outreach campaign, synthesised the replies, and reviewed the tools people already use.

## The short answer

- **As framed today,** a simple subscription tracker is a vitamin: a convenience, not a must-have.
- **The signal:** a 4.0% reply rate, led by PMs and founders, but only 2 booked calls.
- **The way forward:** shift from "tracking subscriptions" to "automating savings toward a life goal" for goal-oriented tech professionals.

Vitamin versus painkiller isn't fixed by the market. It depends on product choices: actionability plus a goal-based framing can turn the same utility into a must-have.

## Interest drops off sharply before the call

| Stage | Count | Rate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contacts targeted (LinkedIn, tech and finance in India) | 577 | |
| Replies | 23 | 4.0%, strong for cold outreach |
| Engaged | 15 | 65% of replies; the other 8 raised objections |
| Calls booked | 2 | 8.7% of replies |

The hook starts conversations, but the pain isn't sharp enough for most people to give up 15 minutes. That's the first sign that, as framed, this is a vitamin.

> People will share an opinion. Few will give it 15 minutes.

The rest of the research is about that gap between intellectual interest and personal urgency.

## Who engaged

- **Product managers** gave the richest feedback. They went beyond anecdotes to market size, competitors and what turns a vitamin into a painkiller. One immediately asked for data validating the problem; another sized the market unprompted and named INDmoney as a competitor.
- **Founders** gave high-level takes on business viability and positioning.
- **Engineers** gave personal, habit-based feedback, often skeptical of how severe the problem is.

PMs are a "meta-aware" beachhead. They feel the problem and can name it, so they're both the best sounding board and the likeliest early adopters.

## The painkiller case: five reasons it could be a must-have

1. **Unnoticed auto-renewals.** Forgotten renewals and unused subscriptions are a real, event-driven loss.
2. **Building financial discipline.** Small charges "snowball over time" and are noticed only when it's too late.
3. **Frictionless payments.** Since UPI, millennial and Gen Z spending has risen, which makes a manager more necessary.
4. **Actionability.** Showing data is a vitamin; finding leaks, nudging and cancelling for you is a must-have.
5. **Ecosystem effects.** A two-sided platform for users and vendors could become indispensable.

The actionability point, from a PM, is the most useful one. It reads as a product roadmap: from a passive dashboard to an active agent working on the user's behalf.

## The vitamin case: three reasons it's only nice to have

1. **User apathy.** Young professionals don't think about long-term goals, or simply don't care to track.
2. **Good-enough tools.** UPI apps already manage mandates; Notion or a sheet works; INDmoney does it as a super app.
3. **Small amounts.** The leak is "normal" money for the middle class, and many people have only a few subscriptions.

One engineer put it plainly: "Vitamin for me. Because I don't have many subscriptions for now myself. Only 3." That hints at a threshold effect: the pain is felt only once recurring payments pile up.

## The real challenge is an awareness gap

> Users see a $10 charge, not the $600 it costs over five years.

The split isn't random. People who called it a painkiller think about second-order consequences. People who called it a vitamin focus on the first-order job of tracking, and think existing tools are enough. The app has to make the long-term cost tangible and tie it to a goal people already care about.

## Each objection sets a bar the product must clear

| Objection | What it demands of the product |
| --- | --- |
| "Existing tools are good enough" | A 10x better proposition: a far simpler experience, or capabilities incumbents can't match |
| "The problem isn't big enough" | Niche down to people with many subscriptions, or reframe around large, emotional goals |
| "Users lack the motivation" | Tap motivation users already have by linking every action to their own goal |

An app can't create motivation where none exists, but it can lower the friction for people who already have it: saving for a down payment, a wedding or early retirement.

## The market has a gap in the middle

| | Proposed app | UPI AutoPay | INDmoney | Spreadsheet / Notion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Detection | Automated via SMS, email or linking | Manual, per app | Automated via aggregation | Fully manual entry |
| Single view | All recurring costs in one place | No, fragmented per app | Yes, in a complex investing UI | Yes, if user-maintained |
| Insight | Finds leaks, shows long-term cost | None | Basic; investment-focused | Only with manual formulas |
| Action | Smart nudges, one-click actions | Pause or revoke a mandate | Limited, view-only | None; act elsewhere |
| Goals | Savings linked to life goals | None | Investment goals only | Manual, complex setup |
| User effort | Low: set up once | Medium | Medium | High |

UPI AutoPay is infrastructure, not a competitor: the opportunity is an intelligence layer on top of it. INDmoney validates automated aggregation, but its breadth is its weakness for this one job. Spreadsheet users prove the pain is real for motivated people, and they are the people to convert.

<small>Sources: NPCI/BHIM, PhonePe, Paytm, INDmoney, NextLeap, Reddit r/Notion.</small>

## Strategy 1: move from passive tracking to active management

- **Smart nudges.** "You haven't used Service X in 90 days. Cancel it and save $15 a month?"
- **One-click cancel.** Aspirational but powerful: handle cancellation for the user and remove the inertia.
- **Duplicate alerts.** Flag overlapping services, such as two music platforms, and prompt to consolidate.

The product shifts from historian to agent.

## Strategy 2: sell the destination, not the vehicle

| Today: vitamin | Proposed: painkiller |
| --- | --- |
| "Track your subscriptions" | "Buy your first home sooner" |
| Onboarding starts by linking accounts | Onboarding starts by setting a goal; the dashboard is a progress bar |
| "You saved $20 by cancelling a subscription." | "You're on track to reach your vacation goal a month earlier!" |

A founder offered the analogy: test automation is a vitamin when sold as a process, but a painkiller when sold as more releases and more revenue. Two other respondents independently said the app becomes a painkiller only when it's tied to an urgent financial goal.

## Strategy 3: niche down to the "Conscious Optimizer"

The ideal customer is defined by mindset, not just demographics:

- **Aged 25–35, facing big costs:** solid income, saving for a home, wedding, children or FIRE.
- **Already managing money:** uses a spreadsheet or app and knows its limits.
- **Financially literate:** understands compounding and opportunity cost.
- **Analytical by profession:** PMs, founders and data scientists who look for systems.

Not the target: people with three subscriptions, or who shrug off small expenses. They don't feel the pain.

## Verdict: the hypothesis holds, with critical qualifications

The original hypothesis was that young tech professionals struggle to track the small spending leaks that hold back their long-term financial goals. That's real and widely acknowledged, but low in intensity for the broad segment.

- **High risk: a simple subscription tracker.** For broad "young tech professionals" it's a vitamin, up against apathy and good-enough alternatives.
- **The opportunity: a goal-oriented optimisation engine.** For Conscious Optimizers, turning small savings into faster progress on big goals is a painkiller.

## Recommendations

1. **Value proposition.** Retire "subscription tracker"; reposition as a Goal Accelerator or Financial Waste Eliminator.
2. **Customer profile.** Use the Conscious Optimizer across all research, marketing and design docs.
3. **Core journey.** Replace Track → View with a loop where every step serves the user's goal: set a goal → discover leaks → take action → see the impact on the goal.
4. **Use the two booked calls to test the new proposition,** not to re-validate the old problem:
   - *Goal validation:* are you saving for a specific large purchase or goal right now, like a down payment or a car?
   - *Pain quantification:* recall a surprise renewal. Was it a minor annoyance or a real setback to your plans?
   - *Solution framing:* App A lists your subscriptions. App B shows your dream trip is three months closer if you cut two unused services. Which is more compelling, and why?
   - *Actionability value:* if an app found a forgotten subscription and cancelled it in one tap, how valuable would that be, from 1 to 10?

## Next: de-risk in three steps

1. **Synthesise.** After both calls, write a one-page concept: proposition, persona, core journey.
2. **Validate.** A landing page plus LinkedIn ads to PMs in India. Success metric: email sign-up rate.
3. **Scope the MVP.** The leanest product that delivers the painkiller promise to Conscious Optimizers only, deliberately ignoring features for the broader, less-motivated market.
