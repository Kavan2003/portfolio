---
title: "“It’s just a stirrer and an induction”"
description: "We see the thing in front of us and miss the thing that makes it work."
date: 2026-11-01
---

The product I work on is a cooking robot. It spent years in R&D before it reached people’s kitchens, and only in the last couple of years has it found its users.

I said this to a friend, an outsider, and he said: what’s tough about that product? It’s just a stirrer and an induction.

I just wanted to drag him into my mental court of justice and prove how wrong he is.

Then the same thought came to me. He works at a company, and one of the products he works on is e-invoicing software. The first thing that came to my mind was: it’s just a PDF maker, what’s tough in that?

Oo damn. I did the same thing.

The thing is, we see the PDF, but we miss the complex regional and country tax laws, the structures of companies, and the compliance that this software brings with it.

Think of n8n. It’s easy to say Claude can do it all. But n8n has one thing AI finds very hard: reliability and consistency, along with observability. You may not understand the tech and code, but n8n shows it all in a graph, and that is powerful. You can understand, not just hope it will work.

And the cooking robot. What “stirrer and induction” misses is an AI model that consistently detects, for example, the shape and cut of tomatoes. There is a model that does just one thing: what size is the tomato, or is it puree? It’s not a ChatGPT wrapper. It’s made from the ground up with deep learning.

→ Every time, we see the thing in front of us and miss the thing that makes it work.

→ Later I found a [paper from Yale, 2002](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3062901/#S59). People rated how well they understood everyday things like zippers and flush toilets, then tried to explain step by step how they work, and their ratings dropped. The researchers called it the illusion of explanatory depth.

Both the illusion and the underconfidence that follows it are things people often have. I see it as an opportunity rather than a weakness. I’ve started an exercise: notice things that are in the background most of the time, and then try to disassemble them in my head.
